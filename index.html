Introduction

04



Diarrhea: May be defined as the passage of loose watery stools3 times or more in a day.

Clinical Types (Part 1)

05

Acute Watery Diarrhoea

Duration: Lasts < 14 days.

Sign: Loose, watery stool passed ≥ 3 times in 24 hours without blood.

Often accompanied by low-grade fever and early vomiting.

Invasive Diarrhoea

Etiology: Bacterial mucosal invasion.

Sign: Patient passes frequent loose stools along with blood.

Associated with fever, abdominal cramp, pallor, and tenesmus.

Clinical Types (Part 2)

06

Persistent Diarrhoea

  • Duration: Occurs acutely (with or without blood) and lasts for ≥ 14 days. With no > 2 intervening days without diarrhoea.

Etiology of Acute Infective Diarrhea

07

Causative Agents

  • Viral: Rotavirus (most common), Enteric Adenovirus, Astrovirus.
  • Bacterial:
    Vibrio cholerae Shigella spp. Salmonella spp. Campylobacter jejuni Escherichia coli
  • Parasitic: Giardia lamblia, Entamoeba histolytica, Cryptosporidium.

Pathogenesis of Rota Viral Diarrhea

08

Route: feco-oral; incubation period: 1-3 days.

After entry, the virus infects, kills & destroy the mature absorptive enterocytes. The damaged cells as well as their affected villi are rapidly replaced by immature brush border & no brush border enzymes e.g. disaccaridase (lactase).

Ingestion of rota virus particle
Infection of absorptive intestinal villus cells
Blunting of tip cell of villi of intestine
↓ Absorption of fluid & electrolytes
Net fluid secretion.
Dehydration and Diselectrolytaemia
↓ Breakdown of disaccharide
CHO malabsorption
stimulation of enteric NS
Osmotic diarrhea

Pathogenesis of Cholera

09

Route of entry: Oral.

Incubation period: few hours to 5 days, usually 2-3 days.

Transmitted by fecal contamination of water & food (approximately 1million must be ingested because organism is particularly sensitive to stomach acid )
Adheres to cells of the brush border of the gut , then colonize & multiplies & secretes enterotoxin called choleragen. Choleragen consists of A subunit & B subunit
A subunit is inserted into the cytosol where it catalyses the addition of ADP –ribose to the Gs protein (Gs stimulatory G protein ). B subunit binds to a ganglioside receptor on the surface of the enterocyte
Stimulation of Adenylyl cyclase
Overproduction of cAMP
Activates cAMP dependent protein kinase
NaCl efflux in the Gut lumen along with water
Massive watery diarrhea

Clinical Features

10

General Signs

  • Diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain.
  • Mild diarrhoea in most cases.
  • In 1-2% cases, severe diarrhea (Severe cholera) occurs.
  • Sudden onset of massive, frequent, watery stools (rice-water stools), containing mucous but no pus.

Within 2-3 hours

Tremendous loss of fluid & electrolytes results in:

  • Life-threatening dehydration
  • Hypochloremia and hypokalaemia
  • Marked weakness and circulatory collapse

Clinical Assessment & Stool

11

Assessment History

  • Vomiting: Note frequency & amount. (First symptom of rotavirus).
  • Diarrhea & Dysentery: Note duration, frequency, consistency, amount, and blood.
  • Fever:Screaming with pale appearance.
  • Intake/Output: Last urine output, feeding and fluid intake.
  • History: Treatments taken, epidemiological links.

Stool Observations

Observe the sample visually to check for:

  • Consistency (watery / mucoid)
  • Colour and Odour
  • Presence of blood

Physical Examination

12

1. Signs of Dehydration

2. Abdominal Distension

3. Severe Malnutrition

Complications

13

Metabolic

  • Electrolyte Imbalance: Hypernatremia, Hyponatremia, Hypokalemia.
  • Hypoglycemia: Widespread in severely malnourished/young infants.
  • Acidosis: Severe bicarbonate depletion combined with systemic shock.

Organ Specific

  • Acute Renal Failure: Systemic circulatory collapse reducing critical renal blood pathways.
  • Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome: Induced via specific cellular path of nephrotoxins from S. Dysenteriae or E. coli.

Investigations

16

Stool Examination

  • Stool RME: No RBC, Pus cells, macrophage.
  • Stool for V. cholera.
  • Stool C/S: To see the growth of any organism.

Blood & Others

  • Blood for CBC, PBF: Low Hb, changes in WBC.
  • S Electrolytes: Low Na, K, Cl, HCO3.
  • Others: e.g. S. Creatinine, X-ray abdomen.

Treatment Overview

17

Essential Elements

  • Rehydration
  • Continued feeding
  • Zinc supplementation
  • Antibiotics (if due to V. cholerae/bacteria)

Other Managements

  • Correction of electrolyte imbalance & metabolic acidosis if present.
  • Treatment of complications (paralytic ileus, AKI, HUS).

Plan A: Home Management

18

Prevent Dehydration

  • Signs: Not enough signs for some/severe.
  • Fluid Choice: ORS, chira pani, rice water, yogurt.
  • Danger Signs: Return if baby drinks poorly, becomes sicker, or has blood in stool.

Amount after each stool:

50-100 mL (< 2 yrs)
100-200 mL (≥ 2 yrs)

Zinc & Nutritional Therapy

23

Zinc Therapy

Once daily for 10-14 days.

  • < 6 months: 10 mg/day
  • ≥ 6 months: 20 mg/day

Role of Zinc

  • Decrease duration severity and recurrence
  • immune modulator
  • Increase Cellular growth and differentiation
  • Increase transport of electrolytes and water
  • Decrease cAMP mediated secreation

    • Continued Feeding

    • < 6 months: Exclusive breastfeeding.
    • ≥ 6 months: Breast milk + nutrient-dense foods.

    Antibiotics Therapy

    Rota Diarrhoea

    Antibiotic Not Indicated

    Cholera

    Use any one of the following:

    • Tetracycline: 12.5 mg/kg 6 hourly for 3 days
    • Azithromycin: 10 mg/kg/day OD for 5 days
    • Ciprofloxacin: 20 mg/kg/day 12 hourly for 5 days
    • Cotrimoxazole: TMP 10 mg/kg/day 12 hourly for 3 days

    Prevention

    25

    Preventive Measures

    • Exclusive Breastfeeding (EBF) & proper weaning practice.
    • Safe drinking water & preventing food/water contamination.
    • Hand washing & improving knowledge of personal hygiene.
    • Rotavirus vaccination.
